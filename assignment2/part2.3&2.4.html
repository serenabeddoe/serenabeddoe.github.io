Part 2.3: Gaussian and Laplacian Stacks

Building the Stacks

This is a breakdown of how I built the gaussian and laplacian stacks:

  1. Building Gaussian Stack

    I read each image and then divided it by 255 so that the pixel values are between 0 and 1 only. The original image is the first level of the stack. For every sima in sigma = [2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64], I made a Gaussian filter with cv2.getGaussianKernel and the outer product gaussian_2d = gaussian_1d @ gaussian_1d.T, using ksize = 6σ + 1. I convolved each colour channel of the previous level with this filter using signal.convolve(..., mode='same'), stacked the channels back together and added the result to the stack. I doubled sigma at each level to give 7 levels that get blurrier each time.

  2. Building Laplacian Stack

    Each Laplacian level is one Gaussian level minus the next one, gaussian_stack[i] - gaussian_stack[i+1], which keeps only the band of frequencies that was removed by that blur. For the last level I used the last Gaussian level, so that adding all of the Laplacian levels together gives back the original image.

  3. Displaying Stacks

    The Laplacian levels have negative values, so I added 0.5 to every level except the last one and clipped the result to 0–1 (so 0 would be mid-gray). I also flipped the channels with [:,:,::-1] since OpenCV reads images as BGR and matplotlib displays them as RGB.

Part 2.4: Multiresolution Bleding

Using a Mask

This is a breakdown of how I used a mask:

  1. Making the Mask

    The mask is the same size as the images and has 1s where I want the first image and 0s where I want the second image. For a vertical seam like for the oraple and my capybara example, the mask is a step function with 1s on the left half and 0s on the right half.

  2. Building Gaussian Stack of the Mask

    I built a Gaussian stack of the mask with the same sigma values as the images.

  3. Masking Each Level

    At every level, I multiplied the first image's Laplacian level by that level of the mask, and the second image's Laplacian level by 1 - mask. Then I added the two masked levels together.

  4. Adding the Levels

    I added all of the combined levels together to get the final blended image. Fine details are blended with a sharp mask and only mix near the seam, while the blurry levels are blended with a very wide mask and mix across a large area.

I tried using 5 levels at first, but the colours still changed too suddenly across the seam. Going up to 7 levels (sigma up to 64) made the colour transition much smoother.

Cutoff Frequencies

I adjusted the sigma values (and subsequently the ksize values) to test whether a lower cutoff frequency (bigger sigma) or higher cutoff frequency (smaller sigma) was better for each set of images. I wanted to make sure that the amount of detail I keep from using both filters is balanced enough that the hybrid image looks appropriate. I did 5 runs where I tested 3 simga values for each of Derek and Nutmeg.

Here are the results from each run:

Derek sigma (low-pass)Nutmeg sigma (high-pass)Result
sigma=6sigma=6Derek's eyes, nose and mouth are still sharp up close and compete with Nutmeg.
sigma=12sigma=2Nutmeg is faint up close, mostly just whiskers and fur texture over a blurry Derek.
sigma=12sigma=6Nutmeg clearly dominates up close and Derek is recognizable from far away.
sigma=12sigma=12Nutmeg is very strong up close, but her stripes and edges still show through from far away.
sigma=20sigma=6Derek is too blurry to recognize well from far away.

I chose sigma=12 for Derek and simga=6 for Nutmeg because it gave the best balance.

To see whether colour works better for the high-frequency component, the low-frequency component, or both, I made the Marko hybrid four times, converting the low-frequency image, the high-frequency image, both, or neither to grayscale. Sigma values stayed fixed. Here are my results:

With "colour for both" and "colour for only the low frequencies" versions, the hybrid images look very similar and relatively natural, with Marko's skin tone, green shirt and the warm background. With the "colour for only the high frequencies" version, the hybrid looks almost completely grayscale and is very similar to the "no colour" version. The only colour left is faint tinting along some edges since the high-frequency image is mostly fine edges and texture and doesn't contain much colour information. I think that the best hybrid image is the "colour for the low frequencies" version since it looks natural, and it is slightly cleaner than the "colour for both" version since it removes the coloured fringes on the edges.
